ALB6. Lipids

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Introduction | Three Important Lipids
  • Lipids include triglycerides, phospholipids, and cholesterol.
  • Their structures determine their roles in energy storage, cell membranes, and biological synthesis.
Triglycerides | Structure
  • A triglyceride consists of one glycerol backbone joined to three fatty acid tails.
  • Each fatty acid contains a hydrocarbon chain made mainly of carbon and hydrogen.
  • Saturated fatty acids contain only single carbon-carbon bonds; unsaturated fatty acids contain one or more carbon-carbon double bonds.
Triglycerides | Energy Storage
  • Triglycerides store energy in animals, plants, and some bacteria.
  • Their long hydrocarbon chains contain many carbon-hydrogen bonds that can release energy when broken.
  • Their low mass-to-energy ratio allows a large amount of energy to be stored in a small space.
  • Because triglycerides are large, non-polar, and insoluble in water, they do not affect the water balance of cells.
Triglycerides | Synthesis by Condensation
  • A triglyceride is synthesised through condensation reactions between glycerol and three fatty acids.
  • The three hydroxyl groups of glycerol react with the carboxyl groups of the fatty acids.
  • Three ester bonds form, producing one triglyceride and releasing three water molecules.
Triglycerides | Breakdown by Hydrolysis
  • Hydrolysis is the reverse of condensation.
  • Three water molecules are added to break the three ester bonds in a triglyceride.
  • The products are one glycerol molecule and three fatty acids.
Phospholipids | Structure and Polarity
  • A phospholipid has a glycerol backbone, two fatty acid tails, and one phosphate group.
  • The glycerol-phosphate head is polar and hydrophilic, so it is attracted to water.
  • The fatty acid tails are non-polar and hydrophobic, so they repel water.
  • Having both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions makes a phospholipid amphipathic.
Phospholipids | Bilayers and Cell Membranes
  • In an aqueous environment, phospholipids form a bilayer: the heads face the water and the tails face inward.
  • The bilayer separates the cytoplasm from the extracellular fluid.
  • Its hydrophobic interior restricts the passage of many water-soluble substances.
  • Therefore, the phospholipid bilayer helps regulate movement into and out of cells.
Cholesterol | Structure
  • Cholesterol is a sterol with four fused carbon rings and a branched hydrocarbon chain.
  • Most of the molecule is non-polar and hydrophobic.
  • Its hydroxyl group is polar and hydrophilic, so cholesterol has both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions.
Cholesterol | Biological Functions
  • Cholesterol is used to make vitamin D, steroid hormones, and bile.
  • It also controls the stability and fluidity of animal cell membranes.
  • Its hydrophilic end aligns with phospholipid heads, while its hydrophobic region aligns with fatty acid tails.
Cholesterol | Temperature and Membrane Fluidity
  • At high temperatures, cholesterol restrains phospholipid movement, reduces excessive fluidity, and stabilises the membrane.
  • At low temperatures, cholesterol prevents phospholipids from packing too closely, helping the membrane remain fluid.
  • Cholesterol therefore acts as a fluidity buffer across temperature changes.
