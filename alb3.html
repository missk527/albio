ALB3. Polysaccharides

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Introduction | What Polysaccharides Are
  • Polysaccharides are complex carbohydrates made from many monosaccharides linked by glycosidic bonds.
  • Common examples include starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
  • They have two major roles: storing energy and providing structural support.
Starch | Forms and Monomers
  • Starch is the main glucose-storage polysaccharide in plants.
  • It occurs in two forms: amylose and amylopectin.
  • Both forms are built from alpha-glucose monomers joined by glycosidic bonds.
Amylose | Structure
  • Amylose is an unbranched chain containing alpha-1,4 glycosidic bonds.
  • An alpha-1,4 bond forms between carbon 1 of one glucose and carbon 4 of the next.
  • The bond angles cause the chain to coil into a compact helical shape.
Amylopectin | Structure
  • Amylopectin is branched because it contains both alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6 glycosidic bonds.
  • Alpha-1,4 bonds form the main chains, while alpha-1,6 bonds create side branches.
  • Its many branch ends allow enzymes to release glucose quickly.
Starch | Adaptations for Storage
  • Starch is insoluble, so it does not affect the water balance of plant cells.
  • It is large, so it does not readily diffuse out of cells.
  • Coiled amylose is compact, allowing much glucose to be stored in a small space.
  • Branched amylopectin provides many accessible ends for rapid hydrolysis and respiration.
Glycogen | Structure and Function
  • Glycogen is the main glucose-storage polysaccharide in animals.
  • It is made from alpha-glucose with alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6 glycosidic bonds.
  • Glycogen is insoluble, large, compact, and more highly branched than amylopectin.
  • Extra branching provides many ends, enabling rapid glucose release for active animals.
Cellulose | Structure
  • Cellulose is made from beta-glucose monomers joined by beta-1,4 glycosidic bonds.
  • Every second beta-glucose monomer is inverted so the hydroxyl groups on carbons 1 and 4 can react.
  • This arrangement produces long, straight, unbranched cellulose chains.
Cellulose | Microfibrils and Strength
  • Hydrogen bonds form cross-links between adjacent cellulose chains.
  • Many cross-linked chains form a strong bundle called a microfibril.
  • Several microfibrils group together to form a macrofibril.
  • This organisation gives plant cell walls rigidity and high tensile strength.
Comparison | Starch, Glycogen, and Cellulose
  • Starch and cellulose occur in plants, whereas glycogen occurs in animals.
  • Starch and glycogen contain alpha-glucose; cellulose contains beta-glucose.
  • Amylose and cellulose are unbranched, while amylopectin and glycogen are branched.
  • Amylose has alpha-1,4 bonds; amylopectin and glycogen have alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6 bonds; cellulose has beta-1,4 bonds.
  • Starch and glycogen store energy, while cellulose provides structural support.
