Notes

1. Structure of nucleotides

A nucleotide is the monomer of nucleic acids. Each nucleotide contains three components:

  • A pentose sugar with five carbon atoms.
  • A nitrogenous base containing carbon and nitrogen.
  • A phosphate group.

2. Polynucleotides and the sugar-phosphate backbone

  • Nucleotides join to form a polynucleotide, a long polymer chain.
  • A phosphodiester bond links the phosphate group of one nucleotide to the sugar of another.
  • Alternating sugars and phosphates form the sugar-phosphate backbone.
  • In the simplified condensation model used in the lesson, water is released when a linkage forms.
  • Hydrolysis uses water to break phosphodiester bonds, breaking the chain into smaller units.
  • A nucleic acid consists of one or more polynucleotide chains. DNA and RNA are nucleic acids.

In cells, nucleic acid synthesis uses activated nucleotide triphosphates and releases pyrophosphate; the condensation description above is the simplified model presented in the speech.

3. DNA structure and bases

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. It usually consists of two polynucleotide strands twisted into a double helix.

  • The sugar in DNA is deoxyribose.
  • The four bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G).
  • Purines: adenine and guanine are larger bases with two fused rings.
  • Pyrimidines: cytosine and thymine are smaller bases with one ring.
  • The rings contain both carbon and nitrogen atoms.

4. Complementary base pairing and antiparallel strands

A–T: 2 hydrogen bonds   |   C–G: 3 hydrogen bonds
  • Complementary base pairing means that A pairs specifically with T, and C pairs specifically with G.
  • Each pair contains one purine and one pyrimidine, maintaining a roughly constant distance between the backbones.
  • The two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′ to 3′, while the other runs 3′ to 5′.
  • Hydrogen bonds connect complementary bases on opposite strands; covalent phosphodiester bonds connect nucleotides within each strand.

5. Functions and useful features of DNA

DNA stores genetic information in its base sequence, including instructions for making proteins. It can be replicated and inherited, passing information between generations.

  • Protected bases: the bases lie inside the double helix, with the sugar-phosphate backbones on the outside.
  • Two template strands: each strand can guide the formation of a new complementary strand during replication.
  • Specific pairing: complementary base pairing helps ensure accurate copying.
  • Large information capacity: long DNA molecules contain many bases and can encode substantial information.
  • Compact storage: the helix and further coiling and packaging allow long DNA molecules to fit inside cells.
  • Strand separation: individually weak hydrogen bonds can be broken during replication without breaking the covalent backbones.

6. RNA structure and function

RNA stands for ribonucleic acid. Its main role emphasized here is helping use DNA information to synthesize proteins.

  • RNA nucleotides contain ribose, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base.
  • RNA contains A, U, C and G; uracil (U) replaces thymine.
  • Uracil is a pyrimidine.
  • RNA is usually single-stranded and shorter than DNA.
  • Both DNA and RNA are nucleotide polymers with sugar-phosphate backbones.

DNA usually has deoxyribose, thymine and two strands; RNA usually has ribose, uracil and one strand. Single-stranded RNA can fold and form locally base-paired regions.
