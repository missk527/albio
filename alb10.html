ALB10. ATP Structure and Function

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

ATP Structure
  • ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate. It is a nucleotide that transfers energy within cells.
  • ATP contains the nitrogenous base adenine, the five-carbon sugar ribose and three phosphate groups.
  • Adenosine is adenine joined to ribose; triphosphate means three phosphates. ADP, adenosine diphosphate, has two phosphate groups.
ATP Synthesis
  • ATP is formed when ADP combines with inorganic phosphate (Pi): ADP + Pi + energy → ATP + water.
  • ATP synthase catalyses this energy-requiring condensation reaction. A bond forms between phosphate groups and water is released.
  • ATP can be regenerated repeatedly from ADP and Pi.
ATP Hydrolysis
  • ATP hydrolase catalyses hydrolysis: ATP + water → ADP + Pi + energy.
  • Water is used to break the bond to the outermost phosphate group, releasing usable energy.
  • The synthesis and hydrolysis reactions together form a renewable ATP cycle.
Cellular Uses of ATP
  • ATP supplies energy for muscle contraction and movement of cells such as sperm.
  • It drives active transport against a concentration gradient, including uptake of mineral ions by plant roots.
  • ATP supports synthesis of large molecules such as proteins and secretion of substances such as hormones.
  • ATP can donate a phosphate group to another molecule. Phosphorylation can activate enzymes by changing their activity.
Why ATP Is an Immediate Energy Source
  • Each ATP hydrolysis releases a manageable amount of energy, limiting waste as heat.
  • ATP releases energy in a single step and can be resynthesised rapidly.
  • ATP is soluble and can move through cells to where energy is needed.
  • ATP is suited to immediate energy transfer rather than long-term energy storage.
