ALB8. Protein Structure

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Overview | Four Levels of Protein Structure
  • Proteins have four structural levels: primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary.
  • These levels interact to determine each protein's unique shape, properties, and function.
  • Each level is defined by a particular arrangement and the bonds or interactions that stabilize it.
Primary Structure | Amino-Acid Sequence
  • Primary structure is the specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain.
  • Adjacent amino acids are joined by strong covalent peptide bonds.
  • The amino-acid sequence provides the foundation for all higher levels of protein structure.
Secondary Structure | Hydrogen Bonding
  • Secondary structure forms when parts of a polypeptide backbone coil or fold in regular patterns.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between backbone amino groups and carboxyl groups in different parts of the chain.
  • The two major forms are the alpha helix and the beta-pleated sheet.
Alpha Helix | Coiled Arrangement
  • An alpha helix is a regularly coiled section of a polypeptide chain.
  • Hydrogen bonds between amino acids at different positions stabilize the coil.
  • The repeated bonding pattern makes this secondary structure relatively predictable.
Beta-Pleated Sheet | Folded Arrangement
  • A beta-pleated sheet has a folded, zigzag arrangement rather than a coil.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between different sections of the polypeptide backbone.
  • Like the alpha helix, its repetitive pattern is more predictable than tertiary folding.
Tertiary Structure | Unique 3D Shape
  • Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional folding and twisting of one polypeptide chain.
  • It is stabilized by interactions between amino-acid R groups.
  • This complex, specific folding gives a protein its biological function.
Tertiary Structure | Stabilizing Interactions
  • Hydrogen bonds are weak individually but can be strong collectively.
  • Ionic bonds form between oppositely charged R groups.
  • Disulfide bridges form between sulfur-containing R groups.
  • Hydrophobic and hydrophilic interactions arise from non-polar and polar R groups.
Quaternary Structure | Multiple Polypeptides
  • Quaternary structure occurs only when two or more polypeptide chains associate.
  • The subunits are held together by interactions similar to those stabilizing tertiary structure.
  • These include hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges, and hydrophobic or hydrophilic interactions.
  • Proteins made from a single polypeptide chain do not have quaternary structure.
Prosthetic Groups | Non-Protein Components
  • Some complex proteins also contain prosthetic groups attached to their polypeptide chains.
  • A prosthetic group is a non-protein component, such as a carbohydrate or lipid.
  • Prosthetic groups can contribute to the structure and function of the complete protein.
