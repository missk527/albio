Notes

1. What is DNA replication?

DNA replication is the process by which DNA makes a copy of itself before cell division. The amount of DNA doubles so that each daughter cell can receive a complete copy of the genetic information.

Replication is semiconservative: each daughter DNA molecule contains one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesized strand.

1 DNA molecule → 2 DNA molecules
Each: 1 original strand + 1 new strand

2. Unwinding and strand separation

DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and separates its strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. It does not break the phosphodiester bonds of the original backbones.

Each exposed original strand acts as a template for a new complementary strand. The region where the DNA opens is called a replication fork.

3. Complementary base pairing

Free DNA nucleotides pair with the exposed bases on each template strand according to complementary base-pairing rules.

  • Adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T): two hydrogen bonds.
  • Guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C): three hydrogen bonds.
  • The template base sequence determines the sequence of the new complementary strand.
Template: 3′-ATGC-5′
New strand: 5′-TACG-3′

4. DNA polymerase and the backbone

DNA polymerase extends a new strand by catalyzing the formation of phosphodiester bonds between adjacent nucleotides. These bonds form the sugar-phosphate backbone.

  • Hydrogen bonds: between complementary bases on opposite strands.
  • Phosphodiester bonds: between nucleotides within the backbone of one strand.
  • DNA polymerase adds nucleotides to the 3′ end of a growing strand. Therefore, new DNA is synthesized 5′ → 3′, while the template is read 3′ → 5′.

Clarification: 5′ → 3′ describes the direction of new-strand synthesis, not the direction in which polymerase reads its template. Polymerase extends an existing end; it does not simply begin at a bare 5′ end. A primer supplies the starting 3′ end.

5. Leading and lagging strands

The two DNA strands are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions. Because polymerase synthesizes only 5′ → 3′, the two new strands are made differently at a replication fork.

  • Leading strand: synthesized continuously in the same direction as the fork advances.
  • Lagging strand: synthesized discontinuously as short sections called Okazaki fragments.
  • Each Okazaki fragment is synthesized 5′ → 3′, growing away from the advancing fork.
  • As helicase exposes more template, additional fragments can be initiated near the fork and extended.
Both strands: synthesis is always 5′ → 3′

6. Joining fragments and completing replication

DNA ligase joins adjacent Okazaki fragments by sealing nicks in the sugar-phosphate backbone with phosphodiester bonds. Primer sequences must first be removed and replaced with DNA.

The leading strand is not built from Okazaki fragments, so it does not require repeated joining of these fragments. This does not mean ligase has no possible role in other completion or repair steps.

After accurate replication, the two daughter DNA molecules have the same base sequence and genetic information as the original. Each contains one parental strand and one new strand.
