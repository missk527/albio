ALB5. Lipids

Key Notes (Synced with Video)

Structure of Lipids
  • Lipids are an important group of biological molecules containing carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.
  • They contain much less oxygen than carbohydrates.
  • Most lipids consist of fatty acids combined with an alcohol.
  • A triglyceride contains three fatty acid tails joined to glycerol.
  • Lipids are not made from long chains of repeating monomers, so they are not true polymers.
Fats and Oils
  • Fats and oils are types of lipid whose properties depend on their fatty acids.
  • Fats are solid at room temperature, whereas oils are liquid at room temperature.
Energy Storage and Cell Membranes
  • Lipids are major energy-storage molecules because their oxidation releases a large amount of energy.
  • Phospholipids are essential structural components of cell membranes.
Waterproofing, Insulation, and Protection
  • Lipids are insoluble in water, so they can form water-resistant barriers.
  • Fat provides thermal insulation and helps organisms retain heat.
  • The lipid layer around nerve cells acts as an electrical insulator and supports nerve impulse transmission.
  • Fat around organs cushions them and protects them from physical damage.
Basic Structure of a Fatty Acid
  • A fatty acid contains a carboxyl group attached to an R group.
  • The R group is a hydrocarbon chain that can vary in length and structure.
Saturated Fatty Acids
  • Saturated fatty acids are fully saturated with hydrogen and contain no carbon–carbon double bonds.
  • Their straight hydrocarbon chains can pack closely together.
  • Strong packing gives lipids rich in saturated fatty acids higher melting points, so they are often solid at room temperature.
Unsaturated Fatty Acids
  • Unsaturated fatty acids contain at least one carbon–carbon double bond.
  • A monounsaturated fatty acid has one carbon–carbon double bond; a polyunsaturated fatty acid has two or more.
  • Double bonds create kinks in the hydrocarbon chains, preventing close packing.
  • Lipids rich in unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points and are often liquid at room temperature.
The Emulsion Test for Lipids
  • Place 2 cm3 of the food sample in a test tube and add 2 cm3 of ethanol.
  • Shake the mixture so that any lipids dissolve in the ethanol.
  • Add 2 cm3 of distilled water.
  • A milky-white emulsion is a positive result for lipids; a clear solution is a negative result.
  • The emulsion forms because lipids are insoluble in water and separate into tiny droplets that scatter light.
